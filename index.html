      01 — The problem

      The books drift, silently.

      There is no error message. Nothing turns red. The two sides of the ledger simply stop agreeing, a little more each month.

      Context

      A mid-market merchant collecting online runs two to four payment aggregators at once, plus marketplaces and cash-on-delivery partners. Money leaves the customer on Day 0 and reaches the bank somewhere between T+1 and T+7 — net of MDR, 18% GST on that MDR, refunds, chargebacks and a rolling reserve.

      Reconciliation happens in a spreadsheet. Four to eight settlement files get downloaded and matched by hand, every day. And critically, the matching happens at batch level, not transaction level — so an individual break doesn't fail loudly, it gets absorbed into a total that still looks approximately right.

      Meanwhile nobody re-prices the aggregator's invoice against the contract. The MDR bill arrives and is paid on trust.

      2–4payment aggregators running in parallel at a typical mid-market merchantSourced
      T+1 → T+7days between the customer paying and the cash actually landing
      0.3–1.2%of online GMV sitting in breaks that have never been explainedSourced

      The pain is invisible until it is large. There is no error message — the books simply drift.ReconOS — the problem, stated plainly

      04 — The insight

      The merchant is auditing the wrong thing.

      The turn

      Everyone treats reconciliation as a volume problem — too many files, not enough hours. That framing leads you to build a faster spreadsheet.

      It is actually a pricing problem. The single place where real money is silently lost is the fee itself: the aggregator computes MDR by card type, network, issuer, MCC, EMI flag and international status, invoices a total, and the merchant pays it. Nobody re-derives that number from the contract, transaction by transaction.

      Which means the honest version of the product isn't "reconciliation software." It's a system that can finish this sentence with a figure: you were overcharged ₹X last year. Everything else follows from that — the pricing model, the sales motion, even the sequencing of what gets built.

      It also explains why the incumbent can't do it. A merchant cannot rely on the same party that charges the MDR to independently audit that MDR. That conflict is structural, and it is the reason the gap stays open.

      05 — The fix

      A closed loop instead of a spreadsheet.

      Before / after

      Today

      • 4–8 files reconciled manually, every day
      • Breaks found at month-end, if at all
      • The MDR invoice is accepted on trust
      • 3–5 days lost at close
      • 1–2 full-time analysts on matching

      With ReconOS

      • Automated three-way match: order → aggregator settlement → bank credit
      • Breaks surfaced same-day, classified and assigned to an owner
      • Every transaction re-priced against the contracted rate card
      • Close compresses; entries export into Tally / Zoho
      • One analyst, working exceptions only

      Three-way match, daily, at transaction level.

      Every break lands in one of eleven fixed categories, each carrying an owner, an ageing clock, an amount at risk and a dispute note. Select a category to see what it looks like on a single transaction.

      07 — The metric decision

      The number I refused to optimise.

      The obvious north star for a reconciliation product is match rate. It is also the one that would make the product dangerous.

      Rejected / chosen

      Rejected

      Match rate — the share of transactions the engine successfully matches

      It rewards exactly the wrong behaviour. A system chasing this number will loosen its thresholds and match records that shouldn't be matched. A false match is worse than no match: it takes a real financial break and buries it under a green tick, and the merchant now believes something untrue about their own money.

      Chosen

      Reconciled Value with Explainable Outcome — the share of processed payment value that is either matched, or assigned a validated exception with an explicit reason

      An honest exception scores the same as a clean match. That single change means the engine has nothing to gain from guessing, so the product's incentive and the customer's interest point the same way. It is why the matching hierarchy puts fuzzy matching last, and why FR-04 is written as "low-confidence records remain exceptions."

      The system must prefer an explicit exception over an unsafe match.ReconOS PRD — critical rule, matching engine

      95%of eligible transactions reconciled automatically or with a deterministic match reasonTarget
      <1%false-positive match rate — the constraint the north star exists to protectTarget
      100%of unmatched records surfaced into an exception queue rather than absorbedTarget

      Case 02 — Product requirements & trade-off design

      Smart Routing Engine

      A gateway that was the right choice this morning can be the wrong one this afternoon. Static routing rules cannot see that happen — and the cost of not seeing it shows up as failed payments, in real time.

      My role

      Problem framing, PRD, success and guardrail metrics, scope boundaries

      Output

      Product requirements document for an MVP routing engine

      Users

      Enterprise merchants running multiple gateways; payment ops and finance teams

      Prototype

      Open the live build ↗

      01 — The problem

      "Send all Visa transactions to Gateway A."

      That is what routing usually looks like. It is a rule written once, on the day it was true.

      Framing

      Gateway performance moves — bank outages, network issues, gateway-level failures. A static rule keeps sending traffic into a degraded path until a human notices and intervenes. By then the merchant has already lost the transactions.

      Written as a user problem rather than a system complaint: a merchant wants each transaction routed to the gateway that gives the best expected outcome, judged on success rate, cost and availability together — not on a preference recorded months ago.

      The interesting part isn't the routing. It's that "best expected outcome" contains a trade-off the merchant, not the engine, has to own.

      02 — The trade-off

      Success rate and cost pull in opposite directions.

      The best-performing gateway is usually the most expensive one. Move the weighting and watch what the engine picks — then degrade a gateway and watch what a static rule would have done instead.

      03 — Metrics

      Four things to improve. Four things not to break.

      The guardrails matter more than the targets. Without them, the engine can hit every success-rate goal by routing everything to the most expensive gateway available.

      Success / guardrail

      Success metrics

      • Payment success rate improves by ≥ 2 percentage points
      • Routing-related payment failures fall by ≥ 10%
      • ≥ 50% of eligible transactions routed through the engine within three months
      • Manual routing interventions fall by ≥ 30%
      Target

      Guardrail metrics

      • Average processing cost per transaction — the price of a higher success rate
      • Payment latency — a routing decision that arrives late is a failed checkout
      • Merchant complaints — the signal that shows up before the metrics do
      • Incorrect routing incidents — the engine doing the wrong thing confidently
      Target

      04 — Non-goals

      Six things this deliberately does not do.

      Every one of these was a reasonable request. Writing them down as non-goals is what stops an MVP becoming a platform before it has shipped once.

      Out of scope

      Onboard new payment gateways automatically

      A separate integration problem with its own failure modes.

      Negotiate gateway pricing

      Commercial work, not a routing decision.

      Build a payment gateway

      The engine chooses between gateways; it does not become one.

      Optimise fraud detection

      Different signals, different owners, different consequences for a false positive.

      Change merchant business rules automatically

      The merchant owns their preferences. The engine works inside them.

      Support every payment method at launch

      Breadth before reliability is how routing engines lose trust.

      A product manager should say what and why, and let engineering determine how.The reason this PRD names no algorithm, language or database

      05 — Edge cases

      The happy path is one line. The rest is the product.

      Customer pays, gateway selected, payment succeeds. Everything below is what has to be decided before launch, not after the first incident.

      Defined behaviour

      No eligible gateway

      Nothing in the pool passes the merchant's rules. Fail gracefully or fall back — but the choice is explicit.

      Identical scores

      Two gateways tie. A deterministic tiebreak, so the same inputs always produce the same route.

      Performance data missing

      A gateway with no recent history cannot be scored. Does it get a default, or get excluded?

      Gateway drops mid-decision

      Availability changes between scoring and routing. The window is small and real.

      Merchant has disabled a gateway

      A manual override always outranks the engine's ranking.

      Amount exceeds threshold

      High-value transactions may need a different route regardless of score.

      The routing decision misses its latency budget

      Wait, use a default gateway, or fail the transaction? This is the edge case that decides whether the engine is safe to put in the checkout path at all — which is why the maximum acceptable routing latency is one of the five open questions the PRD leaves on the table for engineering and the business to answer together, rather than assuming.

      Where it came from

      I shipped this once before I specified it.

      Origin

      At PhonePe I designed merchant routing logic across Cards and NetBanking that delivered ₹8 crore in annualised savings, and migrated 95% of payment traffic across acquiring banks in three months — through phased routing shifts with live monitoring, because moving traffic quickly and moving it safely are different problems.

      That work is why this PRD is written the way it is. The guardrail metrics exist because I have watched a routing change improve one number and quietly damage another. The edge cases are not hypothetical; most of them are things I have seen happen.

      01 — The reframe

      Not a browsing problem.

      The turn

      Every accommodation platform in India competes on inventory. More listings, more filters, more results. That framing produces the experience everyone already hates: four hundred rows, a weekend of phone calls, and a decision made out of exhaustion.

      This is a matching problem, not a browsing problem.The single decision the rest of the product follows from

      Once you accept that, most of the conventional product disappears. You don't need a filter panel, because filters ask the user to translate their life into fields. You don't need four hundred results, because a good match makes the other 390 noise. What you need instead is enough understanding of the person to produce five to ten options and a reason for each one.

      And you need to be right about which signals matter — which is a harder question than it looks, because the ones people search on are not the ones that determine whether they are still happy three months later.

Work-from-home frequency, because it decides whether the room needs to function as an office. Quiet versus social, because it is the single most common reason people leave a shared home early — and the one nobody puts in a search box.

      It is collected conversationally rather than as a form, so a user can answer in their own words and the system extracts the structure. That is the part where the AI does real work: not generating anything, just reading intent well enough that the person doesn't have to think in database fields.

      A number without a reason is just another sorting trick.

      Every match carries an explanation, and the explanation is what makes it usable — because the user is choosing a home they have not seen. Switch profile to see the weighting change, and with it the ranking.

      04 — The journey

      Nine steps, and only two of them are search.

      Most of the value sits after the decision — which is exactly where every existing platform hands the user back to WhatsApp and a PDF.

      End to end

      1

      Conversational onboarding

      A guided, chat-style set of questions covering profile, location anchor, budget, dates, room preferences, food, lifestyle and safety. Answers can be typed naturally; the system builds a living preference profile from them.

      2

      Personalised match feed

      The home screen opens with five to ten properties, each carrying a match score and a one-line reason. Tapping the score opens the full breakdown.

      3

      Smart search and refinement

      "Pet-friendly place near a metro station" re-ranks the feed instantly. Every adjustment teaches the engine more about the user.

      4

      Property deep-dive

      Photos, video walkthrough and 360° tour, full rent and deposit breakdown with hidden charges called out, house rules, commute estimates, internet speed, power and water details, live occupancy, verified resident reviews, and an honest pros-and-cons summary.

      5

      Shortlist, visit and chat

      Side-by-side comparison, a booked visit slot or virtual tour, and a direct conversation with the property manager inside the app.

      6

      Agreement review

      The moment of maximum exposure, and the reason this product exists in its current shape. Detailed below.

      7

      KYC, signing and payment

      Identity verification, digital signing and the deposit or first month's rent in one guided sequence, with the receipt and signed agreement stored in the app.

      8

      Move-in

      A personalised checklist covering documents, essentials, utility setup and key handover — so day one is not improvised.

      9

      Living, and after

      Maintenance tickets, rent reminders, utility tracking, the resident community, a local marketplace, referrals. When the stay ends, the stored preference profile makes the next search almost instant.

      05 — The insight

      The riskiest moment is not the search. It's the signature.

      Agreement assistant

      Walk the journey and ask where a first-time renter is most exposed. It isn't shortlisting. It is the moment they are handed an eleven-month rental agreement written in language designed for someone else's protection, and asked to sign it — usually on the spot, usually without anyone to ask.

      No existing platform helps there. Portals end at the lead. Brokers are on the other side of the table. That gap is the most defensible thing in this product, and it costs almost nothing to occupy.

      What it does

      • Explains each clause in plain language
      • Flags risky terms before signature, not after
      • Surfaces hidden charges the listing didn't mention
      • Summarises notice period and refund policy
      • Points out what the document conspicuously doesn't say

      Why it's the right wedge

      It is the only step in the journey where the user is genuinely frightened and genuinely alone. Solve that and you earn the right to the rest of the relationship — the payment, the maintenance tickets, the next move. Solve search brilliantly and you have built a better version of what already exists.

      06 — What this doesn't have

      No users, no metrics, no validation yet.

      This is a product concept with a working prototype. Presenting it as anything more would be the same kind of flattering number I spend the rest of this portfolio arguing against.

      Honest state

      What exists

      A defined problem, a preference model, a full user journey, and a live prototype anyone can open and click through.

      What doesn't

      Real users, real properties, retention data, supply-side commitments, or any evidence that the match score predicts satisfaction.

      The one I'd test first

      Not whether people want better matching — they obviously do. Whether property operators will submit to verification. Without verified supply the match score is confident and wrong, which is worse than a listings page that never promised anything.

      Launch would focus on dense student and early-career clusters — Delhi NCR and Bengaluru first, then Pune, Hyderabad and Mumbai — on the reasoning that migration volume is highest there and the existing search experience is worst.

      02 — The opportunity

      Real, but urban, southern, and barely started.

      Half the market is one region, three-quarters of it is city-dwellers, and 70% of India barely drinks coffee yet. That gap is the whole opportunity — and it is not a gap you fill with more cafés.

      Market shape

      How big, 2026

      • ₹8,200 Cr café market, growing ~18% a year — set to double by 2028
      • 6,110 → 10,000 branded coffee shops, 2026 to 2030 (13% CAGR)
      • 7–10% yearly growth in domestic coffee consumption
      • 45% of the market is instant — the mass base most brands ignore

      Where it actually is

      • ~50% of demand is South India, on deep filter-coffee culture
      • ~73% of volume is urban; rural is ~27%, mostly southern
      • 70% of India is a rural belt where coffee is still an acquired taste

      03 — The model

      One bean, two businesses.

      The coffee is identical until the last step. Then it splits into a shop you sit in or a packet you take home — and the two make money in opposite ways.

      04 — Follow the money

      Same year. Same filings. Opposite outcomes.

      Line up the best café operator against the best packaged player. One is clawing out of losses. The other prints ₹388 crore of profit.

      FY26

      Starbucks is the interesting half of this. Revenue grew 7% and the loss healed from ₹136 Cr to ₹49 Cr — genuine progress. But look at how it healed: food, gifting and same-store growth, with store expansion deliberately slowed.

      It did not fix the café by opening more cafés. Even the best café operator in the country only works when it slows down and bolts on other revenue. That is the answer to the question we opened with.

      05 — The underrated anchor

      India's most valuable coffee company sells almost none of it in cafés.

      CCL is the thesis compressed into one company: let B2B fund the brand, capture the customer through the pantry, let packaged margins compound — with no real estate to subsidise.

      The shape

      1

      Supplies the world

      World's largest instant-coffee exporter, into 90+ countries. Cost-plus, low-risk, and it funds everything else.

      2

      Builds its own brand

      Continental is the No.3 instant brand in India. The branded arm is now EBITDA-positive and self-funding.

      3

      Premiumises the cup

      Rides the freeze-dried upgrade of mass instant — a ₹3,500 Cr market growing ~11%. Online is 20–25% of branded sales.

      4

      Becomes an FMCG

      ₹4,466 Cr revenue, ₹388 Cr profit, debt cut by ₹700 Cr. Expanding into snacks, iced tea and the UK.

      The café-first brands are quietly migrating toward exactly this shape. Blue Tokai runs 175+ cafés alongside D2C; Sleepy Owl is at-home first and near break-even. That is the direction of travel.

      06 — The call

      Back the cupboard, not the café.

      A market map that doesn't end in a position is a summary. Here is the position, and what it costs to be wrong about it.

      Conviction

      We'd back

      • Picks-and-shovels plus mass premiumisation. The CCL/Continental shape — B2B cash funding a branded at-home arm. Premium instant is the ₹3,500 Cr prize hiding in plain sight.
      • Omnichannel brands with a real home P&L. Blue Tokai, Sleepy Owl — cafés as brand media, margin captured on the shelf and on quick-commerce.

      We'd avoid

      • Pure-play café chains. Structurally hard. Even Starbucks only healed by slowing down and adding food. No café-only chain has IPO'd successfully; Café Coffee Day is the cautionary tale.
      • The crowded premium-D2C middle. 45% of brands share one positioning inside a ₹70–186 Cr pond, with cooling momentum. Being profitable in a small pond isn't the prize.

      Quick-commerce is the new shelf

      Blinkit and Zepto give any brand national reach with zero stores — the distribution advantage cafés used to hold.

      Tax tilts to packaged

      Dine-in sits at 5% with no input credit; packets sit in the goods regime. The regulation leans the same way the economics do.

      AI compounds the home model

      In cafés AI is a cost lever — staffing, stock, forecasting. At home it is a growth lever: targeting and re-order prediction. It makes the capital-light side better, faster.

      Momentum is store-led, margin is home-led — and over three years, margin wins the argument.The bottom line, stated as a bet

      07 — Method

      How the numbers were handled.

      This belongs on the page rather than in a footnote, because in a sector where every aggregator publishes a different market size, the sourcing standard is the analysis.

      Standard

      The rules

      • Load-bearing numbers come from company filings, not coverage of filings
      • Market sizes are cited with source and year, and shown as a range where analysts disagree
      • Directional signals are labelled as directional
      • A single AI-generated number is never a source

      What that changes

      It is why the two companies compared on the previous section are from the same financial year at the same scale, drawn from their own filings — rather than the more convenient pairing of a fresh valuation against an old revenue figure.       08 — The shop floor

      The same question, from three feet instead of thirty thousand.

      Arguing about where café margin goes is one altitude. Here is the other one: the automation I built so a single coffee shop stops losing money to stockouts and manual counting. Press play to watch a real order move through it.

      Automation

      Scenario · a customer orders 3 × Cappuccino

      Why it isn't just a stock counter

      An order is placed in units a customer understands — three cappuccinos. Inventory lives in units a supplier understands — grams of beans, litres of milk. The workflow explodes the recipe in between, so one sale correctly decrements several ingredients at their own rates.

      One digest, not five emails

      Alerts aggregate. If an order pushes three ingredients below threshold, the owner gets one message listing all three — each with the suggested reorder quantity, the supplier, and a reply-ready reorder note they can send as-is.

      What happens when it breaks

      Every write step carries an error branch. If the inventory update fails, a human is emailed the product, the quantity, the affected ingredient record and the actual error — because a silent automation failure in stock control is worse than no automation at all.

      Built on Airtable and Make. Two scenarios in production: orders-to-stock-alert (shown above) and stock-in-to-inventory for deliveries. The failure branch is the part I'd argue matters most — it is the difference between a demo and something an owner can rely on.
