      01 — What changed

      The first version of this was too big to build.

      It was specified for mid-market merchants running several payment aggregators at once. Correct about the problem, wrong about who to start with.

      The pivot

      The original scope was a transaction-level reconciliation platform for merchants doing ₹100 Cr of online GMV. The problem was real and the sizing held up. Two things about it did not.

      First, that buyer already has a finance team, and a finance team is a slow, committee-shaped sale. Second, and more serious: for that merchant the gateway is only half the leak. In Indian D2C the courier takes more money than the gateway does, through COD remittances that arrive short, shipping charged above the rate card, return charges on orders that were delivered, and weight disputes with a deadline nobody is tracking.

      So the scope moved. One platform instead of many, Shopify, where the order data is already clean and exportable. One buyer instead of a department, the founder, who feels the money directly. And both sides of the leak, courier and gateway, because fixing one and ignoring the other is how a tool gets described as useful but not worth paying for.

      Before

      Mid-market merchants, gateway reconciliation

      Two to four payment aggregators, a finance team as the buyer, a platform to be specified and then built.

      Now

      D2C brands on Shopify, courier and gateway

      One platform, the founder as the buyer, a working product with a free first audit.

      Narrowing the buyer is what made it buildable. The problem did not get smaller, the starting point did.ReconOS — why the scope moved

      02 — The problem

      Two partners deduct, nobody re-prices.

      There is no error message. The payout is simply smaller than it should be, by an amount small enough per order to never be worth checking by hand.

      Context

      A brand sells an order. The gateway takes a fee and 18% GST on that fee, then settles a net amount days later. The courier charges for shipping by zone and weight, adds a COD fee, and remits the cash it collected on its own schedule. Every one of those numbers was agreed in a rate card, and none of them is checked against it.

      A founder cannot audit this by hand because the evidence is spread across four or five different files that use different column names for the same thing, and because catching it requires re-pricing every order rather than scanning a total. A ₹14 overcharge on one shipment is invisible. Across a month of orders it is a salary.

      04 — The decision worth defending

      Claim and Check are different words on purpose.

      A tool that tells a founder to go and argue with their courier is only useful if it is right. The cost of a wrong finding is not a wrong number on a screen, it is a founder losing an argument with a partner they depend on.

      The rule

      Claim

      The brand's own files prove it

      The rate card says one number, the invoice says another, and both are in the evidence. This goes in the claim email.

      Check

      Something does not add up

      Worth a look, but not provable from the files alone. Aggregator rate gaps land here, because their prices vary by the courier underneath.

      The same instinct drives how ambiguity is priced. When a gateway file does not say whether a payment was credit or debit, the fee is checked against the highest rate it could have been. That deliberately under-claims. A founder who sends a claim and gets told the number is wrong does not send a second one, and the product has then cost them something rather than found them something.

      Precision matters more than coverage here. Finding less, and being right about it, is the product.ReconOS — on what gets called a claim

      05 — The adoption problem

      Rate cards are compulsory, and nobody types them in.

      The audit cannot run without them, and asking a founder to enter zone-and-weight pricing for four partners is where a tool like this quietly dies.

      The answer

      So ReconOS does not ask. It fills each rate card from what the brand was actually charged most often, and it will read the real one from whatever form it exists in: a PDF addendum, a screenshot or a photo of a page, an Excel sheet, or text pasted out of an email. Screenshots are read on the device, so the file still never leaves the browser.

      Two things then matter. Every value shows where it came from, so a number inferred from invoices is never mistaken for a number from the agreement. And where the agreement and the actual charges disagree, that gap is itself flagged, because it is often the finding. The founder confirms before the audit runs.

      06 — A constraint taken seriously

      The files never leave the browser.

      Why

      These exports hold a brand's full order history, customer addresses, and its commercial terms with every partner. Uploading that to a server to run an audit creates an obligation that an early product has no business taking on, and it is the first objection a careful founder raises.

      Everything is parsed and checked in the browser. There is no upload and nothing is stored, which also means there is no breach to have.

      07 — Pricing

      The first audit is free, because the pitch is the number it finds.

      Nobody pays a subscription to discover whether they have a problem. They pay once they have seen the figure.

      Plans

      ₹0Free audit. Every check, on one month of files, before anything is paid.
      ₹999Starter, per month. For brands up to around 2,000 orders a month.
      ₹2,499Growth, per month. Every partner, and weekly audits through sale season.

      Deliberately a flat subscription rather than a share of what is recovered. A percentage model sounds aligned and is not: it rewards the tool for finding big claims rather than correct ones, which is the opposite of the Claim-and-Check rule the whole product rests on.

      08 — How it is proven

      A fake brand with leaks hidden in it on purpose.

      Testing

      The site opens on an invented brand, Indigo Loom, with a realistic month of data: two gateways, two couriers, and a known set of planted leaks. Anyone can explore every screen before uploading a thing, which removes the usual demand that a visitor hand over their commercial data to find out whether the product is any good.

      It is also the test suite. 57 tests run against that month, and they fail in both directions: if a planted leak is missed, and if a clean order is flagged. A reconciliation tool that finds things which are not there is worse than no tool, because it spends the founder's credibility with their own partners.

      Status, stated plainly

      ReconOS is in early access. The product and the site are built and the engine is tested, but there are no customers and no recovered amounts to report yet. Every figure in the live build is from the sample brand, not from a real one.

      01 — The problem

      Every merchant waits on a bank. Nobody could say for how long, or why.

      This is how MID procurement works across Indian acquiring, generalised from operating it. It is not any one employer's internal process, and every acquirer named on this page is invented.

      Context

      A payment aggregator cannot put a merchant live until an acquiring bank issues a MID — a merchant ID. Every merchant needs its own dedicated MID from each acquirer, and goes to three or four of six or seven acquirers in parallel.

      Roughly a thousand requests a month were run by three people from a Google Sheet updated by hand. A large share bounced back from the bank for missing information, and each bounce cost a full acquirer cycle.

      Turnaround per team was worked out manually every week for a leadership review. It could say a request was slow. It could not say which party the time belonged to.

      03 — The modelling decisions

      Get the unit of work right, and the clocks follow.

      Most of the product is in these decisions. The code that implements them is the easy part.

      Data model

      The unit of work is merchant + MCC + acquirer, not merchant + acquirer. One merchant can run several use cases, each needing its own merchant category code and its own MID request. That is also why bank replies are matched on legal name plus MCC, not name alone.

      A sheet with one row per merchant cannot hold this at all. It is the reason "partially procured" existed as a word with nothing behind it.

      The turnaround rules

      Working days only

      Each acquirer has its own holiday calendar, so the same date range can be a different number of days for different banks.

      A 2pm cut-off

      A batch sent after the cut-off starts the bank's clock the next working day, not the day it was sent.

      Ops time is measured per pickup

      Not merged into one figure. The number of pickups on a request is itself the rework measure.

      Hands-on versus waiting

      Ops time splits into time working the request and time waiting on a bank, so ops efficiency can be read without the bank's queue inside it.

      Clocks overlap in two places

      Ops runs alongside the acquirer, because getting the MID is ops' job while the bank works it. Onboarding runs alongside BD, because onboarding owns the ticket until it comes back clean. So per-party days add up to more than the elapsed time — and a share-of-delay pie chart would be arithmetically wrong. The honest picture is a timeline per request.

      04 — What was built

      Seven parts, built end to end.

      Every figure in this section is measured against a seeded synthetic dataset of 360 merchants, 1,376 requests and 626 bank emails — not live traffic.

      System

      01 · Turnaround engine

      Event-sourced, so nothing is typed in

      Nothing stores "TAT = 5 days". Every figure derives from an immutable event log, so numbers cannot go stale, a rule change recalculates history instead of losing it, and any number opens into its own timeline.

      02 · Bank reply parser

      Reads the replies out of the inbox

      Replies arrive as tables in the email body, numbered lists or attached sheets — each acquirer with its own column names and wording. The parser turns them into events against the right request.

      03 · Intake readiness check

      Catches missing data before a bank does

      Per-acquirer field requirements plus format and cross-field validation, so a request that would bounce is stopped before it costs an acquirer cycle.

      04 · Bank file generation

      Each bank's template, filled and checked

      One submission template per acquirer held as configuration, because banks change them with no notice. Every column declares who owns it, so a blank is reported against the team that owes it rather than counted as an error. Pre-flight catches what a person cannot see by eye: a value one word off a dropdown, a pincode about to lose its leading zero, two tabs describing one merchant differently.

      05 · Restriction registry

      Six category lists that disagree

      Each acquirer keeps its own restricted-category list, in four incompatible formats, and one merchant can be eligible at one bank and refused at another. Held as editable data with the source and date on every entry, because a list only engineers can change is out of date within a month.

      06 · SLA chaser

      Chases what is late, then stops

      Judged on the round the bank is holding now, never the total, and never sent for a request that is actually waiting on us. One nudge, one firmer follow-up, then it stops emailing and hands over to a person.

      06 — Two mistakes

      How it was wrong, and how that was caught.

      This matters more than the results above. A system that reports 99.0% is only worth trusting if you know how it fails.

      Failure modes

      Mistake 1

      The name matcher merged two different companies

      Normalising legal names stripped "India" as noise. It isn't: "Oakhurst Ventures India Private Limited" and "Oakhurst Ventures Pvt Ltd" are different entities. Five bank replies were matched to the wrong request at 1.00 confidence, and nothing flagged it.

      The fix generalises: entity suffixes are safe to canonicalise, including misspelled ones, because they are not the distinctive part of a name. Anything before them is not.

      Mistake 2

      A perfect score, against test data I had written

      After that fix the parser hit 100% with an empty review queue. A perfect score against synthetic mess of one's own invention proves little — and it left the human-review path untested.

      So the test data was made harder on purpose: status wordings outside the vocabulary, and names mangled past recognition. The parser declines all of them rather than guessing.

      A row held for review costs a minute. A row applied to the wrong request writes a false event into the clock, and every figure downstream inherits it.Why the parser is built to decline

      07 — The chase, and whether it works

      Building the chaser was easy. Measuring it was the problem.

      The sequencing put chasing last on purpose: chasing a bank on a number before the number is trusted automates an argument. Once the scorecard was right, the chaser was a week. Then I tried to prove it helped, and could not.

      Measurement

      The obvious comparison says chasing makes banks slower: chased rounds averaged 9.5 working days against 4.4 for unchased ones. That is not an effect, it is the selection. Every chased round had already run past the SLA by definition, and the unchased group is mostly banks that answered on day two.

      So I compared inside each acquirer instead. Also wrong, and the report caught it: the chase goes out the day after each bank's own SLA, so on any given day the chased group is simply the banks with short SLAs. The first version printed four banks with zero chases, which is what gave it away.

      Then the real problem. The chase rule is deterministic, so within any one bank every eligible round was chased. There is no control group. No amount of analysis could answer the question, because everybody eligible had been treated.

      The fix

      A deliberate holdout

      One in ten late applications is never chased, chosen by a stable hash, with the largest merchants excluded. A handful of merchants wait slightly longer, which is the price of knowing whether the other nine tenths of the chasing does anything.

      The current answer

      There isn't one yet

      125 chased rounds against 9 held out, and the largest group is 16 short of a usable floor. The report says that in those words rather than printing a number, because a figure from that evidence is noise a team would act on.

      A chaser that reports a number it cannot support is worse than one that reports nothing, because somebody will put the number in a deck.MID Procurement — on measuring the chase

      There is a test that plants a known two-day effect and checks the estimator recovers it, which is what makes the rest of the module worth believing.

      02 — The problem

      Every merchant waits on a bank. Nobody could say for how long, or why.

      What has to happen before a merchant can take a card payment, and the four ways it fails.

      Context

      A merchant signs with the aggregator. Before a single transaction can be processed, each acquiring bank the merchant will route through has to issue a MID against that merchant's specific line of business. One merchant commonly needs three or four MIDs from a panel of six or seven acquirers, raised in parallel, each with its own forms, its own category rules and its own idea of how long a reply should take.

      Every one of those applications passes between four parties: BD, who owns the merchant relationship and the documents; onboarding, who owns the ticket; operations, who raises it and chases it; and the acquirer, who decides.

      The scale this runs atToday
      Requests a month~1,000
      People handling them3
      Acquirers on the panel6 to 7
      Acquirers per merchant3 to 4, in parallel
      Requests that bounce back for missing or wrong data30 to 40%
      Where it is trackedone spreadsheet, by hand

      Turnaround is unknowable. Leadership asks how long procurement takes. The answer is produced by hand each week by reading a sheet of dates. It cannot be broken down by party without someone rebuilding it, it cannot be opened up when a specific merchant took 22 days, and because the sheet records a status rather than a history, a cell that gets overwritten takes the evidence with it.

      Gaps are found at the worst possible moment. A missing signatory PAN surfaces while someone is filling in a bank's template, which is several ops days after the ticket arrived. The ticket then bounces, and those days are spent twice: once before the bounce and once after it comes back.

      Each bank is a different animal, and the differences are undocumented. Six acquirers means six submission templates, six sets of category rules, six definitions of a reasonable reply time, and six reply formats to read. All of that knowledge lives in the heads of three people.

      Chasing is either forgotten or indiscriminate. There is no list of what is genuinely late, so follow-up is either skipped or done by sending everyone a reminder, which trains banks to ignore them.

      The tracker holds one row per merchant. The actual unit of work is one merchant, one MCC, one acquirer.       05 — The rules that are easy to get wrong

      The part that cannot be derived from first principles by someone who has not run the process.

      Each rule below is encoded as an executable test, so a later change cannot quietly break it.

      Domain rules

      5.1 The unit of work is merchant + MCC + acquirer. Not merchant + acquirer. Bank replies are therefore matched on legal name plus MCC, because a merchant with two live applications at one bank has two different answers coming.

      5.2 Clocks overlap, in two specific places. Ops runs alongside the acquirer, because getting the MID is ops' job and they do not put it down while the bank has it. Onboarding runs alongside BD, because onboarding owns the ticket until it comes back clean. The consequence: per-party days sum to more than elapsed time, so a share-of-delay pie chart is arithmetically wrong no matter how good it looks. The honest visual is a timeline per request with the overlaps drawn.

      5.3 Ops is measured per episode, not merged. A request ops picks up three times is three entries, and that count is itself the rework measure. Raising to a bank splits an episode into solo and overlap phases but is not a new pickup.

      5.4 Ops time splits into solo and overlap. Solo is the efficiency figure. Overlap is time ops is accountable for but does not control. Reporting one without the other misleads in opposite directions.

      5.5 A 2pm cut-off, on the acquirer clock only. A batch sent after 2pm starts the bank's clock the next working day. Internal clocks start immediately.

      5.6 Working days, with per-acquirer holiday calendars. Most acquirers share one; one or two differ.

      5.7 Same-day handoff counts as one day, not zero. A party that handled and passed on a request consumed a day of someone's attention.

      5.8 Deprioritisation stops every clock, and those requests leave the turnaround averages but are counted separately with how long they sat first. That is ops effort which produced nothing, and hiding it flatters the team.

      5.9 Approval and MID delivery are different events on different email threads. The gap between them is invisible in the tracker and sits inside procurement turnaround. Measured here as a separate acquirer phase.

      5.10 Acquirer SLA is judged per response round, not on total held time. A bank that answers in 4 days, asks for a document, then answers in 4 more has met a 5-day SLA twice. Charging it the 8-day sum bills the bank for our own turnaround in between, and a bank that receives a complaint it can disprove stops reading the next one. Total held time is still reported, separately, as a measure of our own data quality: more than one round means the application could not be decided in a single pass.

      5.11 BAU and bulk migration are reported separately. A migration backlog otherwise destroys the BAU averages and hides a real capacity problem behind a one-off event.

      5.12 The merchant is not a party. Time waiting on a merchant is charged to BD, as it is today. Whether that is fair to BD is an open question in section 10.

      06 — What the product does

      Six capabilities, each with the decision worth defending.

      Figures in italics are from the current run of the seeded synthetic dataset.

      6.1 Intake

      Checks every application the moment the ticket lands, at three levels: present, is the field there; well formed, does the PAN match the PAN format and does the GSTIN pass its own checksum; and consistent with the rest of the record.

      A GSTIN carries the entity's PAN at characters 3 to 12 and its issuing state in the first two, so a record can pass every field-level check and still describe two different companies. That contradiction is what an underwriter spots, and finding it that way costs a full acquirer cycle.

      Readiness is decided per acquirer, because acquirers ask for different fields. A missing signatory PAN blocks the three that want it and leaves the fourth untouched, which is what makes partial raising possible.

      Bounces are consolidated one per party, not per acquirer, and the comment names the consequence. "Company GST: not provided (blocks ALDRIDGE, NORTHWAY_CYBS, NORTHWAY_FSS)" tells the receiving team how urgent it is in a way that "missing GST" does not.

      Current run, 390 merchant use cases: 70.3% clean for every acquirer they were raised to, 12.6% ready for some and blocked for others, 17.2% blocked everywhere.

      6.2 Bank files

      One submission template per acquirer, described as configuration rather than code, because banks change these templates with no notice and no version number. The cost of absorbing a change decides whether the file is generated or typed.

      Every column declares who owns it: filled from the record, derived, constant, ops input, or owned by sales or finance. Most of the largest template is not ops', and that is the honest answer to how much of it can be automated. The pre-flight then reports "sales owes a settlement cycle on four merchants", which is a message that can be sent. "17 empty cells" is not.

      Nothing is invented to fill a cell. A plausible value typed in by ops is a commercial term nobody agreed, and it goes live.

      Sheet grain is declared. The identity tabs are one row per merchant, the deal tab one row per application. Writing a two-use-case merchant twice on the identity tab asks the bank to open two accounts for one merchant.

      Identifier columns are written as text. A pincode starting with zero and a twelve-digit account number are both numbers to a spreadsheet, and stored as numbers the bank reads something other than what was reviewed.

      Rows are held back, not the file. Four merchants waiting on a missing value do not keep the other fifty out of the morning batch.

      Current run: 408 requests waiting, 324 go out today, 59 held on documents, 13 blocked by house policy, 12 waiting on an ops decision about a restriction.

      6.3 Reply parser

      Two streams, status replies and MID deliveries, read from each bank's own messy format: different column names, free-text status wording, rejection reasons written as sentences, legal names that drift from the record, and replies that cover only part of a batch.

      Extraction is driven by the shape of the email, not the acquirer. Going by acquirer means a bank that changes its layout silently yields zero rows and nobody notices for a week.

      Negations are checked first. "Not Approved" contains "Approved", and a substring test records a declined merchant as won.

      Ambiguity fails closed. Two candidates within 0.04 of each other go to a person.

      Current run: 1,250 rows from 626 emails, 99.0% applied automatically at 100% precision, 1.0% held for review.

      6.4 Restrictions

      Each acquirer keeps its own restricted-category list. They do not agree with each other and they are not even in the same format: coded lists, lists split by payment instrument, a separate narrower ban for one product, and a page of prose with no codes at all. A merchant on one category can be eligible at one bank, conditional at a second and refused at a third, so this cannot be answered once for the merchant.

      Every entry carries its source, and confidence follows the source rather than being typed in by whoever added it: a published circular is 1.0, a written confirmation 0.95, a rejection actually received 0.9, something said on a call 0.6. Nothing is ever deleted; entries are retired with a date and a reason.

      Overrides are recorded, which is what makes the list self-correcting. An override the bank refuses on the category proves the entry right. One the bank approves proves it stale. One refused for an unrelated reason proves nothing either way and is counted as neither.

      Current registry: 89 live entries across six acquirers, 3 blocking (all house policy), 33 warnings, 53 notes.

      Only our own policy blocks. A category the business will not onboard is a rule. A category one bank currently refuses is a fact about today, and facts about today go stale without telling anyone. A hard block fails invisibly, because the system quietly stops raising to a bank that would now accept, and there is no rejection to notice.The design call this section exists for

      6.5 The chaser

      Finds what each acquirer is holding past the agreed response time, ranks it, drafts one email per acquirer, and stops.

      Nothing is chased that is waiting on us. 572 of the currently open requests are. A request late because a bank asked for a document nobody sent is not theirs to answer for, and chasing it spends credibility needed for the ones that genuinely are late.

      Lateness is the round they hold now, following rule 5.10.

      Ranked by days over, weighted by priority tier, steeply enough that a P0 one day late outranks a P4 six days late, shallowly enough that a P4 twelve days over still outranks that P0, so the queue does not starve its own tail.

      The ladder ends. One nudge, one firmer follow-up, then it stops emailing and writes a note for a person to raise on the relationship call.

      Current run: 119 applications with an acquirer, 36 past the agreed time.

      6.6 Dashboard

      One self-contained page. Blended ops turnaround as the headline, hands-on time only, with the unraised queue excluded and reported separately underneath, because an average that includes an open queue moves with backlog size rather than with how the team is performing.

      Then the acquirer scorecard sorted by bank days so the laggard surfaces on its own, bounce and rejection reasons built from the reason codes, an exception list ranked past-SLA first, and a per-request timeline with the overlapping clocks drawn and elapsed days stated against summed party days, so the gap between those two numbers is explicit rather than hidden.

      10 — Open questions

      Five things this document does not settle.

      Unresolved

      Should the merchant be a party? BD is currently charged for merchant silence. Adding a merchant clock would be fairer to BD and would make every historical comparison discontinuous.

      Were all 117 fields on the largest bank template mandatory, or does a typical merchant fill a fraction? This decides how much of that file can truthfully be called automated.

      What is the right holdout share? 10% answers the chase question in months. 20% answers it in half the time and makes twice as many merchants wait.

      Should contested rejections be a separate state? When ops argues a rejection and the bank reclassifies it as "additional documents required", both the true rejection rate and the document count should stay honest.

      How should the registry handle a bank that loosens a category permanently? Currently a person retires the entry. There is a case for the evidence doing it automatically after enough approvals, and a stronger case against.

      01 — The problem

      "Send all Visa transactions to Gateway A."

      05 — Edge cases

      The happy path is one line. The rest is the product.

      Customer pays, gateway selected, payment succeeds. Everything below is what has to be decided before launch, not after the first incident.

      Defined behaviour

      No eligible gateway

      Nothing in the pool passes the merchant's rules. Fail gracefully or fall back — but the choice is explicit.

      Identical scores

      Two gateways tie. A deterministic tiebreak, so the same inputs always produce the same route.

      Performance data missing

      A gateway with no recent history cannot be scored. Does it get a default, or get excluded?

      Gateway drops mid-decision

      Availability changes between scoring and routing. The window is small and real.

      Merchant has disabled a gateway

      A manual override always outranks the engine's ranking.

      Amount exceeds threshold

      High-value transactions may need a different route regardless of score.

      The routing decision misses its latency budget

      Wait, use a default gateway, or fail the transaction? This is the edge case that decides whether the engine is safe to put in the checkout path at all — which is why the maximum acceptable routing latency is one of the five open questions the PRD leaves on the table for engineering and the business to answer together, rather than assuming.
