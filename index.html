      01 — The problem

      The books drift, silently.

      There is no error message. Nothing turns red. The two sides of the ledger simply stop agreeing, a little more each month.

      Context

      A mid-market merchant collecting online runs two to four payment aggregators at once, plus marketplaces and cash-on-delivery partners. Money leaves the customer on Day 0 and reaches the bank somewhere between T+1 and T+7 — net of MDR, 18% GST on that MDR, refunds, chargebacks and a rolling reserve.

      Reconciliation happens in a spreadsheet. Four to eight settlement files get downloaded and matched by hand, every day. And critically, the matching happens at batch level, not transaction level — so an individual break doesn't fail loudly, it gets absorbed into a total that still looks approximately right.

      Meanwhile nobody re-prices the aggregator's invoice against the contract. The MDR bill arrives and is paid on trust.

      2–4payment aggregators running in parallel at a typical mid-market merchantSourced
      T+1 → T+7days between the customer paying and the cash actually landing
      0.3–1.2%of online GMV sitting in breaks that have never been explainedSourced

      The pain is invisible until it is large. There is no error message — the books simply drift.ReconOS — the problem, stated plainly

      04 — The insight

      The merchant is auditing the wrong thing.

      The turn

      Everyone treats reconciliation as a volume problem — too many files, not enough hours. That framing leads you to build a faster spreadsheet.

      It is actually a pricing problem. The single place where real money is silently lost is the fee itself: the aggregator computes MDR by card type, network, issuer, MCC, EMI flag and international status, invoices a total, and the merchant pays it. Nobody re-derives that number from the contract, transaction by transaction.

      Which means the honest version of the product isn't "reconciliation software." It's a system that can finish this sentence with a figure: you were overcharged ₹X last year. Everything else follows from that — the pricing model, the sales motion, even the sequencing of what gets built.

      It also explains why the incumbent can't do it. A merchant cannot rely on the same party that charges the MDR to independently audit that MDR. That conflict is structural, and it is the reason the gap stays open.

      05 — The fix

      A closed loop instead of a spreadsheet.

      Before / after

      Today

      • 4–8 files reconciled manually, every day
      • Breaks found at month-end, if at all
      • The MDR invoice is accepted on trust
      • 3–5 days lost at close
      • 1–2 full-time analysts on matching

      With ReconOS

      • Automated three-way match: order → aggregator settlement → bank credit
      • Breaks surfaced same-day, classified and assigned to an owner
      • Every transaction re-priced against the contracted rate card
      • Close compresses; entries export into Tally / Zoho
      • One analyst, working exceptions only

      07 — The metric decision

      The number I refused to optimise.

      The obvious north star for a reconciliation product is match rate. It is also the one that would make the product dangerous.

      Rejected / chosen

      Rejected

      Match rate — the share of transactions the engine successfully matches

      It rewards exactly the wrong behaviour. A system chasing this number will loosen its thresholds and match records that shouldn't be matched. A false match is worse than no match: it takes a real financial break and buries it under a green tick, and the merchant now believes something untrue about their own money.

      Chosen

      Reconciled Value with Explainable Outcome — the share of processed payment value that is either matched, or assigned a validated exception with an explicit reason

      An honest exception scores the same as a clean match. That single change means the engine has nothing to gain from guessing, so the product's incentive and the customer's interest point the same way. It is why the matching hierarchy puts fuzzy matching last, and why FR-04 is written as "low-confidence records remain exceptions."

      The system must prefer an explicit exception over an unsafe match.ReconOS PRD — critical rule, matching engine

      95%of eligible transactions reconciled automatically or with a deterministic match reasonTarget
      <1%false-positive match rate — the constraint the north star exists to protectTarget
      100%of unmatched records surfaced into an exception queue rather than absorbedTarget

      01 — The problem

      03 — The modelling decisions

      Get the unit of work right, and the clocks follow.

      Most of the product is in these decisions. The code that implements them is the easy part.

      Data model

      The unit of work is merchant + MCC + acquirer, not merchant + acquirer. One merchant can run several use cases, each needing its own merchant category code and its own MID request. That is also why bank replies are matched on legal name plus MCC, not name alone.

      A sheet with one row per merchant cannot hold this at all. It is the reason "partially procured" existed as a word with nothing behind it.

      The turnaround rules

      Working days only

      Each acquirer has its own holiday calendar, so the same date range can be a different number of days for different banks.

      A 2pm cut-off

      A batch sent after the cut-off starts the bank's clock the next working day, not the day it was sent.

      Ops time is measured per pickup

      Not merged into one figure. The number of pickups on a request is itself the rework measure.

      Hands-on versus waiting

      Ops time splits into time working the request and time waiting on a bank, so ops efficiency can be read without the bank's queue inside it.

      Clocks overlap in two places

      Ops runs alongside the acquirer, because getting the MID is ops' job while the bank works it. Onboarding runs alongside BD, because onboarding owns the ticket until it comes back clean. So per-party days add up to more than the elapsed time — and a share-of-delay pie chart would be arithmetically wrong. The honest picture is a timeline per request.

      Judging the bank

      Rejected

      Acquirer SLA on total time — 4 days, a document request, then 4 more: 8 days against a 5-day SLA

      The sum includes the days between rounds, when the request was back with us gathering the document. Measuring the bank on the total bills it for our own turnaround.

      Chosen

      Acquirer SLA on the slowest single response round — the same bank met a 5-day SLA twice

      Each round is the bank's own time. The days in between belong to ops, and they already show up there as a second pickup.

      04 — What was built

      Four parts, built end to end.

      Every figure in this section is measured against a seeded synthetic dataset of 360 merchants, 1,380 requests and 653 bank emails — not live traffic.

      System

      01 · Turnaround engine

      Event-sourced, so nothing is typed in

      Nothing stores "TAT = 5 days". Every figure derives from an immutable event log, so numbers cannot go stale, a rule change recalculates history instead of losing it, and any number opens into its own timeline.

      02 · Bank reply parser

      Reads the replies out of the inbox

      Replies arrive as tables in the email body, numbered lists or attached sheets — each acquirer with its own column names and wording. The parser turns them into events against the right request.

      03 · Intake readiness check

      Catches missing data before a bank does

      Per-acquirer field requirements plus format and cross-field validation, so a request that would bounce is stopped before it costs an acquirer cycle.

      07 — The research that changed the plan

      Three findings, before any product was built.

      The site was designed around Shopify and WooCommerce sellers on Stripe. Platform and competitor research then showed that segment did not exist in the form assumed.

      Findings

      1

      US Shopify stores can't use Stripe as their gateway

      Where Shopify Payments is available, Stripe isn't offered separately and disputes are handled in the Shopify admin. "Shopify sellers on Stripe" was not a real segment. The product had to integrate with Shopify's own dispute system.

      2

      The free baseline is stronger than assumed

      Shopify already auto-fills basic evidence and auto-submits on the due date, including AI insights for "not received". Stripe's Smart Disputes auto-counters some disputes at a 30% success fee.

      3

      Chargeflow serves 20k+ Shopify merchants at 25% of recovered with no minimum Sourced. The site's claim that such tools are only for large merchants was inaccurate, and was flagged for removal.

      What changed as a result

      • Shopify-first, integrating with Shopify Payments disputes.

      Shopify's API lets an app save dispute evidence — but not submit it

      Save the full package; the seller clicks submit; Shopify auto-submits saved evidence on the due date if they don't

      Case 05 — Product requirements & trade-off design

      RouteIQ

      A gateway that was the right choice this morning can be the wrong one this afternoon. Static routing rules cannot see that happen — and the cost of not seeing it shows up as failed payments, in real time.

      My role

      Problem framing, PRD, success and guardrail metrics, scope boundaries

      Output

      Product requirements document for an MVP routing engine

      Users

      Enterprise merchants running multiple gateways; payment ops and finance teams

      Prototype

      Open the live build ↗

      01 — The problem

      "Send all Visa transactions to Gateway A."

      That is what routing usually looks like. It is a rule written once, on the day it was true.

      Framing

      Gateway performance moves — bank outages, network issues, gateway-level failures. A static rule keeps sending traffic into a degraded path until a human notices and intervenes. By then the merchant has already lost the transactions.

      Written as a user problem rather than a system complaint: a merchant wants each transaction routed to the gateway that gives the best expected outcome, judged on success rate, cost and availability together — not on a preference recorded months ago.

      The interesting part isn't the routing. It's that "best expected outcome" contains a trade-off the merchant, not the engine, has to own.

      02 — The trade-off

      Success rate and cost pull in opposite directions.

      The best-performing gateway is usually the most expensive one. Move the weighting and watch what the engine picks — then degrade a gateway and watch what a static rule would have done instead.

      The routing engine should optimise for merchant business outcomes, not simply maximise payment success rate.RouteIQ PRD — product principle

      03 — Metrics

      Four things to improve. Four things not to break.

      The guardrails matter more than the targets. Without them, the engine can hit every success-rate goal by routing everything to the most expensive gateway available.

      Success / guardrail

      Success metrics

      • Payment success rate improves by ≥ 2 percentage points
      • Routing-related payment failures fall by ≥ 10%
      • ≥ 50% of eligible transactions routed through the engine within three months
      • Manual routing interventions fall by ≥ 30%
      Target

      Guardrail metrics

      • Average processing cost per transaction — the price of a higher success rate
      • Payment latency — a routing decision that arrives late is a failed checkout
      • Merchant complaints — the signal that shows up before the metrics do
      • Incorrect routing incidents — the engine doing the wrong thing confidently
      Target

      04 — Non-goals

      Six things this deliberately does not do.

      Every one of these was a reasonable request. Writing them down as non-goals is what stops an MVP becoming a platform before it has shipped once.

      Out of scope

      Onboard new payment gateways automatically

      A separate integration problem with its own failure modes.

      Negotiate gateway pricing

      Commercial work, not a routing decision.

      Build a payment gateway

      The engine chooses between gateways; it does not become one.

      Optimise fraud detection

      Different signals, different owners, different consequences for a false positive.

      Change merchant business rules automatically

      The merchant owns their preferences. The engine works inside them.

      Support every payment method at launch

      Breadth before reliability is how routing engines lose trust.

      A product manager should say what and why, and let engineering determine how.The reason this PRD names no algorithm, language or database

      05 — Edge cases

      The happy path is one line. The rest is the product.

      Customer pays, gateway selected, payment succeeds. Everything below is what has to be decided before launch, not after the first incident.

      Defined behaviour

      No eligible gateway

      Nothing in the pool passes the merchant's rules. Fail gracefully or fall back — but the choice is explicit.

      Identical scores

      Two gateways tie. A deterministic tiebreak, so the same inputs always produce the same route.

      Performance data missing

      A gateway with no recent history cannot be scored. Does it get a default, or get excluded?

      Gateway drops mid-decision

      Availability changes between scoring and routing. The window is small and real.

      Merchant has disabled a gateway

      A manual override always outranks the engine's ranking.

      Amount exceeds threshold

      High-value transactions may need a different route regardless of score.

      The routing decision misses its latency budget

      Wait, use a default gateway, or fail the transaction? This is the edge case that decides whether the engine is safe to put in the checkout path at all — which is why the maximum acceptable routing latency is one of the five open questions the PRD leaves on the table for engineering and the business to answer together, rather than assuming.

      Where it came from

      I shipped this once before I specified it.

      Origin

      At PhonePe I designed merchant routing logic across Cards and NetBanking that delivered ₹8 crore in annualised savings, and migrated 95% of payment traffic across acquiring banks in three months — through phased routing shifts with live monitoring, because moving traffic quickly and moving it safely are different problems.

      That work is why this PRD is written the way it is. The guardrail metrics exist because I have watched a routing change improve one number and quietly damage another. The edge cases are not hypothetical; most of them are things I have seen happen.

      $1,300

      For competitive roles, switchers and high-pressure loops. Five targeted resumes with continuous tuning, full cover-letter suite, network validation, line-by-line codebase review, three tests and negotiation scripting.

      Underneath the pricing sits the actual product decision: seven modules bundled rather than sold separately — audit, CV and cover letter, LinkedIn, GitHub, interview prep, micro-upskilling and application support. The market's habit is to sell one of these at a time. The bundle only works if you can argue that careers don't fail at one point, which is what the persona work was for.

      Post-purchase was designed alongside it — delivery SLAs, progress tracking and the nurture sequence across all three tiers — because a service business that sells an outcome and then goes quiet churns at renewal.

      04 — Go to market

      Four weeks, one escalating argument.

      The content sequence is a story, not a calendar: establish that the problem is systemic, show that it fails both sides, present the fix, then prove it.

      Launch

      1

      Week 1 · Problem awareness

      The problem is systemic, not personal

      "300 applications. Zero callbacks." "75% of resumes never reach a human." The ATS black hole, geo job-market data, and the AI resume trap.

      2

      Week 2 · System breakdown

      Hiring is failing both sides

      Recruiter inbox overload, recruiter AI fatigue, the doom loop where AI applications meet AI screening. This is the week that earns credibility with recruiters, not just candidates.

      3

      Weeks 3–4 · Launch & proof

      Human-first, then evidence

      Product launch, the three-pillar framework, a founder AMA, then case studies and results — and a cohort-two conversion push once the proof is public.

      LinkedIn — authority

      Both personas. Professional credibility, long-form proof, lead gen and webinars. 70% paid / 30% organic.

      Instagram — demand

      Tech Transitioner. Reach and emotional resonance, creator-driven discovery, career-pivot reels. 80% paid / 20% organic.

      Reddit — trust

      ATS Victim. Career communities where frustrated job seekers already are — so it runs 20% paid / 80% organic, or it gets rejected outright.

      Budget is 65% paid / 35% organic at launch, shifting to 55/45 by month six and 45/55 by month twelve — on the reasoning that paid buys the first audience and organic has to hold it Modelled.
